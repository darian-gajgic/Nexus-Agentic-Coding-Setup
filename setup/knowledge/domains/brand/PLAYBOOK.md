# Brand Playbook

> Scope: brand strategy, naming, verbal identity, visual direction, brand audits, and multi-brand governance for a 2-person team running SaaS, client work, e-commerce, and a music/DJ career.
> Before any brand task: read `~/knowledge/BUSINESS-CONTEXT.md` (which brand, stage, audience, portfolio rules) and `~/knowledge/STYLE-VOICE.md` (house writing rules — brand voice may tighten them, never contradict them).
> Every deliverable is scored against `~/knowledge/domains/brand/RUBRIC.md` before anyone sees it.

## Routing

| Task smells like… | Run playbook | Deliverable format |
|---|---|---|
| "Who is this for / what do we stand for / new brand" | 1. Brand strategy | `examples/brand-onepager.md` |

| "Brief a designer / judge a logo / image direction" | 4. Visual direction | brief + judging notes |

| "Keep 4 brands straight with no overhead" | 6. Governance | rules in this file, applied |

## Operating principles

1. **Position against the customer's real alternative, not "the market."**
   Why: buyers compare you to their current habit — a spreadsheet, a DM thread, doing nothing — not to your competitor list.
2. **Strategy is refusal. A positioning that excludes nobody isn't finished.**
   Why: a claim any company could make triggers no memory and no preference; the named excluded audience is the proof of sharpness.
3. **At small scale, distinctive beats different.**
   Why: nobody researches a small brand — they recognize it or they don't. Recognition is bought with repetition; differentiation only pays after recognition exists.
4. **Voice is rules, not adjectives.**
   Why: "friendly" produces different copy from every writer; "contractions always, exclamation marks never" produces the same brand twice.
5. **One promise per brand.**
   Why: customers keep score on exactly one thing; a second promise halves the odds they remember either.
6. **Consistency compounds; novelty resets the clock.**
   Why: you get bored of your own assets years before the audience even registers them — boredom is usually the signal the brand is starting to work.
7. **Write the brand for the moment of doubt, not the moment of enthusiasm.**
   Why: brand earns its keep at checkout hesitation, error states, and refund emails; the enthusiastic visitor was going to buy anyway.
8. **The founder is not the audience.**
   Why: "I like it" is data about you. Ties are broken by the audience's alternative, never by internal taste.
9. **A brand exists only in shipped touchpoints.**
   Why: strategy documents build nothing; the 40th consistent post, invoice, and error message build the memory structure.
10. **Governance that runs beats governance that's complete.**
    Why: for two people, a one-page sheet used weekly outperforms a 40-page book opened never.

## Task playbooks

### 1. Brand strategy (audience insight → positioning → personality → promise)

1. Read `~/knowledge/BUSINESS-CONTEXT.md`. Confirm which brand this is for and its money goal (subscribe, buy, book). Goal unclear → stop and ask.
2. Define ONE primary audience as a person in a situation, not a demographic.
   - Format: "[role] who [situation] and currently [alternative]."
   - Pass: "wedding photographers drowning in a 3-week editing backlog." Fail: "creatives 25–40."
3. Extract the insight. Write literally: "They currently [alternative], which costs them [time/money/status] and makes them feel [emotion]."
   - Cannot name the current alternative → STOP. Collect 3 real data points first (competitor reviews, forum threads, 2 short interviews), then return.
   - Prefer a scene over a statistic — one observed moment the copy can be built from.
4. Draft positioning with the full template, no fields skipped:
   "For [audience] who [situation/need], [Brand] is the [category] that [single most important benefit], because [reason to believe]. Unlike [primary alternative], we [key difference]."
5. Test against ALL four; rework until all pass:
   - **Swap test:** replace the brand name with the nearest competitor's. Still credibly true? Fail.
   - **Opposite test:** state the opposite of the benefit claim. If no sane company would ever run the opposite ("we make it hard"), the claim is empty. Fail.
   - **Repeat test:** a stranger can say the gist back after one read.
   - **Exclusion test:** who is this NOT for — named, in writing.
6. Personality: exactly 3 traits, each with a "but not" boundary AND one behavior.
   - Format: "Confident but not smug — we state prices without apology."
   - Reject any trait 3+ direct competitors could claim word-for-word, unless the behavior makes it concrete.
7. Promise: one sentence a customer could catch you breaking ("support answers within one business day").
   - Must contain a number, timeframe, or observable behavior.
   - If a breach is unimaginable, it's decoration, not a promise — rework until slightly scary.
8. Trace check: each trait must serve the positioning; the promise must be the positioning made operational. Anything that doesn't trace back gets cut.
9. Deliver as a one-pager (format: `examples/brand-onepager.md`). Run RUBRIC. Any gate fails → revise before showing anyone.

### 2. Naming

1. Fix constraints BEFORE generating:
   - market language(s); standalone brand or sub-brand;
   - where the name must live (domain, app store, storefront, vinyl sleeve, lineup poster);
   - the trademark class it would register in.
2. Generate ≥ 30 candidates across all four lanes — no judging while generating:
   - descriptive (says what it does) · suggestive/metaphor · coined/invented · arbitrary (place, person, object).
3. Shortlist filter — kill a candidate if ANY check fails:
   - **Radio test:** say it aloud once; the listener spells it correctly.
   - **Bar test:** "I run a thing called ___" comes out without apology or a spelling lesson.
   - No negative or awkward meaning in the audience's top 3 languages.
   - Not within one sound or two characters of a known brand in or near the category.
   - Two people pronounce it identically on first read.
4. Practical checks on the final 3–5 — document results in a table with a check date (verify current availability):
   - Domain: exact .com or a clean alternative (get-/use- prefix, .io/.fm if audience-appropriate) (verify current availability).
   - Handles on the 2–3 platforms this brand will actually use (verify current availability).
   - Trademark screen: search the relevant registers (e.g. EUIPO, USPTO) in your class; an identical or confusingly similar LIVE mark in class = kill (verify current availability). This is a screen, not legal advice — actual filing goes to a professional.
   - Search results page 1: who owns the term today? An entrenched page 1 means years of fighting for your own name.
5. Score survivors 0–2 each on: memorability after one day · fit to positioning · practical availability.
   - Highest total wins. Break ties with: "which would you rather say out loud 1,000 times."
6. Cool-off 48 hours. Use the name in 5 real sentences (phone intro, party intro, bio line, invoice header, "I work at ___").
   - Still comfortable → commit, and register domain + handles the same day.

### 3. Verbal identity (voice + tone)

1. Input: the 3 personality traits from the one-pager. No traits yet → do playbook 1 first.
   - Check `~/knowledge/STYLE-VOICE.md`; brand rules may be stricter than house rules, never in conflict.
2. Convert each trait into 1–2 mechanical writing rules. A rule must name something countable or checkable:
   - sentence length, person (we/you), contractions, punctuation, banned words, capitalization.
   - "Sound human" is not a rule; "average ≤ 16 words per sentence in product copy" is.
3. Build the do/don't table, ≥ 5 rows. Each row = rule + one real sentence written both ways.
   - The DON'T must be plausible — something a reasonable writer would actually produce, not a strawman.
4. Word lists: 10 words/phrases we use, 10 we never use.
   - The never-list must include the category's clichés: scan 5 competitor sites; anything appearing on 3+ goes on the banned list.
5. Tone flex map — voice constant, tone flexes. Define ≥ 4 contexts:
   - marketing/social · product/sales page · support/error/failure · money/legal.
   - For each, set 2 dials (e.g. "humor: off; sentences: shorter").
   - Hard rule: the more stressed the reader, the fewer the words and the fewer the jokes — zero jokes in error and money contexts.
6. Blind test: rewrite one real existing paragraph in the new voice. Show old + new unlabeled to the other teammate.
   - They must pick the on-brand one AND point to the rule that makes it so. They can't → rules too weak; tighten step 2.

### 4. Visual identity direction (how a non-designer briefs and judges)

1. Collect 10–15 references BEFORE writing any direction: screenshots of brands adjacent to yours but not competitors.
   - Sort into yes / no / almost. Write one line on every "almost" — the gap between almost and yes is where your direction lives.
2. Write mood as tensions, not adjectives, max 3: "warm but not cute," "technical but not cold."
   - Tensions give a designer or an image model a corridor; a pile of adjectives gives noise.
3. Color: choose a stance, not hexes —
   - temperature (warm/cool), saturation (muted/vivid), value (light/dark);
   - ONE accent with a job ("everything quiet; one hot orange, reserved for actions");
   - then face the category default (fintech blue, eco sage, "premium" black): comply or defect ON PURPOSE — write down which, and why.
4. Type direction in plain words, max 2 typefaces: one display voice ("wide geometric sans" / "editorial serif") + one quiet workhorse for everything else.
5. Imagery: 3 always / 3 never. ("Always real product, hard daylight, visible hands. Never stock handshakes, neon gradients, AI-smooth faces.")
6. The brief is one page, containing exactly:
   - audience + positioning one-liner;
   - the 3 tensions;
   - annotated references (the "almost" notes included);
   - hard constraints: formats needed, budget, deadline, assets that must survive.
   - Send that — never "make it pop."
7. Judge deliveries against the brief, not taste — run all four:
   - **Squint test:** recognizable at thumbnail/24 px?
   - **Grayscale test:** still works with color removed?
   - **Lineup test:** drop it among 5 competitor screenshots — find it in 3 seconds?
   - **In-situ test:** mock it onto the real touchpoint (label, app header, cover art) before approving.
8. Feedback format: "the brief says X, this does Y" — never "I don't like it."
   - Cap revisions at 2 rounds; still off after 2 → the brief was wrong. Fix the brief, don't buy round 3.

### 5. Brand audit (consistency scan across touchpoints)

1. Inventory every live touchpoint from `~/knowledge/BUSINESS-CONTEXT.md` plus a manual sweep:
   - site pages, socials, marketplaces, email flows, transactional emails, invoices/proposals, packaging, press kit/EPK, link-in-bio.
   - List each with URL or location.
2. Capture evidence: screenshot or copy the current state of every touchpoint. Audit from evidence, never from memory.
3. Check each touchpoint against the brand one-pager on 7 axes, pass/fail per axis:
   1. name/logo usage
   2. color
   3. type
   4. voice rules
   5. positioning message present
   6. promise kept
   7. CTA consistency
4. Write findings one line each; grade severity and effort:
   - **S1** — contradicts positioning or breaks the promise (wrong audience, wrong claim, discounting that undercuts a premium position).
   - **S2** — violates a written voice or visual rule (off-palette, banned phrase, off-tone reply).
   - **S3** — cosmetic drift (old logo lockup in a footer, inconsistent capitalization).
   - Effort: quick (< 30 min) or project.
5. Prioritize in this exact order — never open a project for an S3:
   1. all S1, regardless of effort
   2. S2-quick this week
   3. S2-project scheduled with a date
   4. S3 batched into the next natural redesign.
6. Deliver (format: `examples/brand-audit-report.md`):
   - findings table + detailed fixes;
   - top-5 fixes with owner and date;
   - a "what's working — protect it" list.
7. Recurrence: quarterly, timeboxed to 60 minutes on the same touchpoint list.
   - If the audit doesn't fit the box, the brand has too many touchpoints — kill channels, not the timebox.

### 6. Governance for a 2-person, multi-brand team

1. One brand = one one-pager. It is the single source of truth: a rule not on it is not a rule.
   - All one-pagers live in one folder, linked from `~/knowledge/BUSINESS-CONTEXT.md`.
2. Per brand, keep exactly one asset folder containing only:
   - logo (SVG + transparent PNG);
   - palette as a text file (hex list);
   - font names + files/links;
   - 3 approved templates (post, email, doc).
   - Anything else gets deleted or promoted into a template.
3. Portfolio rule — decide once per brand pair, in writing: **connected** (may reference each other) or **sealed** (never cross-post, never share voice).
   - Default: sealed. The DJ persona endorsing the SaaS is a decision, not an accident.
4. Never start from blank: every new touchpoint begins as a copy of an approved template.
   - No template exists → creating the template IS the task; do that first.
5. Change control: editing a one-pager needs both humans' explicit OK plus a one-line dated changelog entry at the bottom of the file.
   - AI agents may PROPOSE edits; they never merge them.
6. Weekly 10-minute cross-check: each human scans the other's shipped output for the week against the relevant one-pager.
   - Drift found → fix in < 30 min now, or log it as an audit finding.
7. Cadence: full audit quarterly (playbook 5); one-pager review twice a year.
   - The review question is "what did we learn," never "what are we bored of" (principle 6).

## Junior mistakes

- **Writing a mission statement and calling it positioning** ("we empower creators to do their best work"). Correction: positioning names an audience, an alternative, and a difference — if it could hang on any company's wall, it isn't positioning.
- **Falling in love with a name before the checks.** Correction: run domain/handle/trademark screens on the whole shortlist first (verify current availability); assign affection last.
- **Voice guides made of adjectives** ("friendly, bold, authentic"). Correction: every adjective becomes a checkable rule with a do/don't pair, or it gets cut.
- **Mood board first, strategy never.** Correction: visuals are playbook 4 and take positioning as input; a beautiful board with no positioning is decoration.
- **Copying big-brand minimalism.** Correction: Apple can afford ambiguity because everyone already knows Apple. A small brand must say what it is — clarity first, cool second.
- **Targeting "everyone who…".** Correction: name one person in one situation. Sharp targeting changes the message, not the checkout — outsiders still buy.
- **Rebranding out of boredom.** Correction: check recognition and engagement data before touching working assets; internal boredom is the default state of a brand that's working.
- **Changing voice per channel.** Correction: tone flexes, voice doesn't — same person, different room. TikTok-you and invoice-you are one person.
- **Benchmarking only against direct competitors.** Correction: audit against the customer's actual alternative too — often "do nothing," which competes on effort, not features.
- **Shipping the strategy doc as the result.** Correction: the deliverable is changed touchpoints; every strategy ends with a list of what gets rewritten or redesigned, by whom, by when.
- **Treating the tagline as poetry.** Correction: a tagline is the positioning compressed — test it by whether a stranger can guess what you sell from it alone.

## Escalate to frontier review when…

Run the deliverable through `cjudge` (frontier second review) BEFORE shipping if ANY is true:

1. A new brand name is about to be committed publicly (registration, announcement, print run) — naming is the most expensive brand mistake to reverse.
2. The work renames or repositions a brand that already has paying customers or an audience above ~1,000 followers.
3. The deliverable makes verifiable claims — health, money, guarantees, "only/first/best" — legal and trust exposure.
4. The output goes directly to a paying client on their deadline without both humans having reviewed it.
5. It touches the music/DJ persona's public identity — artist-brand damage is personal and permanent.
6. The two humans still disagree after one structured debate round (each argues in ≤ 5 sentences against the RUBRIC, not against taste).
7. The RUBRIC verdict is borderline (any gate arguable, or score within 2 points of the pass line) AND shipping is time-pressured.
8. The work leans on cultural references, slang, or humor aimed at a community the team isn't part of — high offense/cringe risk, low internal ability to detect it.

Default otherwise: self-review with RUBRIC.md, teammate spot-check, ship.
