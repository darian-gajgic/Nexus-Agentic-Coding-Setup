# Positioning One-Pager — "ClientLoop"

Product used for illustration: **ClientLoop**, a client-approval portal for tiny web studios.
Produced by `PLAYBOOK.md` → Task playbook 1 (steps referenced inline).
This page is the single source for all copy: homepage, launch posts, demo script, ads. Nothing customer-facing contradicts it.

---

## 1. Category (what is it?)

**Subcategory frame:** "A client approval portal **for freelance web studios**."

- NOT a new category ("collaboration reimagined" — banned by playbook 1 step 6).
- NOT the parent category ("a project management tool") — we lose every feature comparison fought there.
- The frame borrows understanding the buyer already has ("client portal") and narrows it to a group that feels unserved ("for studios like mine").

## 2. ICP (who is it for?)

**Owner of a 1–5 person web/design studio, running 3–10 active client projects, whose client communication currently lives in email threads + WhatsApp + shared Drive links.**

- **Trigger event:** a deliverable was approved verbally or in chat, then disputed later — the studio ate unpaid revision rounds.
- **Observable from outside** (so we can build a prospect list):
  - posts portfolio work on Instagram/Dribbble/Behance,
  - lists "web design / branding" services on their own site,
  - has no /portal or client-login page of their own.
- **Budget reality:** already pays for 2–5 tools in the $10–40/mo band; a $25/mo tool needs no approval process, no procurement.
- **Who it is NOT for:**
  - agencies >10 people — they need roles/permissions we deliberately don't have;
  - internal product teams — no external client, no approval dispute;
  - studios whose clients refuse anything beyond email — we still notify by email, but if the client won't click one link, we can't help.

## 3. Alternatives (what would they use instead?)

| Alternative | What it does well | Where it fails this ICP |
|---|---|---|
| Email + Drive + WhatsApp ("do nothing") | Free, zero client friction, everyone already has it | Approvals buried in threads; no record; "which version?" chaos; disputes end in unpaid work |
| Trello/Notion board shared with the client | Cheap, flexible | Clients get lost in the studio's internal mess; no formal "approve" action; studios stop sharing after one bad experience |
| Basecamp | Real client access, mature product | Priced and shaped for whole-company collaboration; approvals are informal comments; overkill at 1–5 people |
| Full PM suites (ClickUp, Asana + guest seats) | Powerful, studios may already own one | Client-facing views are an afterthought; setup burden lands on the studio; clients need accounts and training |
| Design-proofing point tools | Excellent pixel-level annotation | Cover only design files — not copy docs, staging links, or the whole deliverable flow; yet another silo |

**The biggest competitor is row 1.** The homepage and the demo must beat "just email it", not ClickUp.

## 4. Differentiated value (why us?)

Three claims. Each = capability + who cares + evidence we can show (playbook 1 steps 3–4).

1. **Clients need zero accounts.**
   - Capability: magic-link access to a clean page showing only THEIR project.
   - Attacks the #1 failure of every software alternative: client-side friction.
   - Evidence: pilot clients opened links within a median 3 hours; zero "how do I log in" tickets (illustrative).
2. **Approvals are one click — and on the record.**
   - Capability: timestamped, versioned approval trail, exportable as PDF.
   - Directly answers the trigger event (disputed approvals → unpaid revisions).
   - Evidence: pilot studios recovered an average 1.5 revision rounds per project (illustrative — replace with real pilot data).
3. **A project is set up in under 3 minutes.**
   - Capability: template per service type (site build, brand kit, landing page).
   - Attacks the "setup burden" failure of PM suites.
   - Evidence: recorded first-run median 2m40s across 9 pilot studios (illustrative).

**What we concede (trade-off, stated on purpose):**
No time tracking. No invoicing. No internal task boards. Studios keep their existing tools for those jobs.
We choose depth on the approval loop, which costs us the "replace all my tools" buyer — that buyer churns at our size anyway.

## 6. One-line pitch

**"ClientLoop gives tiny web studios a client-proof approval trail — clients click one link, approve in one click, and 'but I never approved that' never happens again."**

## 7. Homepage headline options

1. **"Never eat another unpaid revision round."**
   - Leads with the money pain from the trigger event.
   - Default choice: a pain the owner has personally felt, phrased in owner language.
2. **"Client approvals your clients will actually use."**
   - Leads with the client-friction differentiator.
   - Choose if interviews show fear-of-annoying-clients outranks money pain.
3. **"The approval trail for 2-person studios."**
   - Leads with ICP self-identification.
   - Choose for community launches, where the reader's first question is "is this for me?".

Subhead under any of the three:
"Send one link. Your client sees the work, comments, and approves — versioned, timestamped, on the record. No client accounts, ever."

## 8. Objection handling (top 4 from interviews and pilot)

- "My clients won't use another tool." → It isn't a tool for them: one link, no signup, works on their phone. Same effort as opening a Drive link.
- "I already have ClickUp." → Keep it. ClickUp runs your studio; ClientLoop faces your client. We do the one thing guest seats do badly.
- "Can't I just get sign-off by email?" → You can — until the thread has 47 messages and 3 file versions. The record exists for the day it's disputed.
- "Is my clients' work private?" → Links are unguessable and expire; export everything anytime ({{FILL: real security/data posture — do not invent claims, gate G8}}).

## Why this works

Annotations for juniors — the load-bearing choices, tied to `PLAYBOOK.md` and `RUBRIC.md`:

1. **Subcategory frame ("X for Y"), not a new category.**
   A 2-person team cannot fund market education. Borrowing "client portal" costs zero explanation; narrowing to "for web studios" creates the "finally, for me" reaction (playbook 1 step 6; principle 2).
2. **The ICP is buildable as a list.**
   Role + size + observable outside markers (Dribbble presence, no /portal page) means we could assemble 100 named prospects this week — the playbook 1 step 5 test and rubric gate G1. "Freelancers" would fail both.
3. **The trigger event does double duty.**
   "Disputed approval → unpaid revisions" defines WHEN they shop AND supplies headline #1. Positioning without a trigger event produces "nice someday" products nobody urgently buys.
4. **"Do nothing" is treated as the market leader.**
   The alternatives table starts with email+Drive, not with VC-funded lookalikes (playbook 1 step 1). For this ICP, inertia — not a rival — is the thing to beat, so the demo script targets it.
5. **Every value claim is falsifiable.**
   Each has a capability plus a number we could turn out to be wrong about (rubric D2). "Seamless collaboration" appears nowhere — kill-list item 2.
6. **A concession is stated in the artifact itself.**
   "No time tracking, no invoicing" tells readers who should NOT buy — which makes the rest believable, and pre-commits the team against all-in-one drift (gate G5; rubric D7; see also `mvp-scope-cut.md` items 13–14 and 20 — scope and positioning must agree).
7. **Illustrative numbers are labeled illustrative.**
   Passes gates G4/G8 while still teaching the SHAPE of good proof: small-n pilot behavior beats fabricated big claims.
8. **Headlines ship as options with selection rules,** not one "best" line.
   The choosing rule (which pain ranked higher in interviews) teaches the decision, so next time the junior can make it alone.
9. **Objections come from the feedback log, not imagination.**
   Playbook 6 step 8 and playbook 7 feed this section; each answer re-uses positioning language instead of inventing new promises.
